```
\section{Consecutive $k$-cycles with inversion}
	We consider permutation graphs generated by \textbf{consecutive   $k$-cycles and their inverses}.
	Consecutive  $k$-cycles of length $k$ are  cyclic permutations: $(i,i+1,i+2, \ldots, i+k-1)$, for $i=1,\ldots,n-k+1$. 
	For example, for $k=2$ they are neighbour transpositions $(i,i+1)$ for $i=1,\ldots,n-1$ - the famous Coxeter generators of $S_n$,
	for $k=3$ they are: 3-cycles  $(i,i+1,i+2)$ for $i=1,\ldots,n-2$ well-known to generate the $A_n$ subgroup of $S_n$. In general,
	for even $k$ the generated subgroup is the entire $S_n$, while for odd $k$ it is $A_n$ (subgroup of even permutations). 
i.e., operations that move an element of a permutation of $n$ elements $k-1$ times to the left or to the right. For example,
for $k=4$ the permutation $(1234)$ moves element 4 three times to the left and 1,2 and 3 once to the right,
while $(4321)$ moves the element 1 three times to the right and the elements 2,3,4 once to the left.
```

# Building D(4,3) Step by Step

Let me walk you through constructing the permutation graph **D(4,3)**, where we have $n=4$ elements and consecutive $k=3$ cycles.

## Step 1: Identify the Generators

For $n=4$ and $k=3$, the consecutive 3-cycles are:
- $(1,2,3)$ for $i=1$
- $(2,3,4)$ for $i=2$

And their inverses:
- $(1,2,3)^{-1} = (3,2,1) = (1,3,2)$
- $(2,3,4)^{-1} = (4,3,2) = (2,4,3)$

**So we have 4 operations total** that generate edges in our graph.

## Step 2: Understand What Each Generator Does

Think of a permutation as an arrangement, say $[a,b,c,d]$:

- **$(1,2,3)$**: Cycles the first 3 positions → $[b,c,a,d]$ 
  - Element at position 1 moves 2 spots right (wrapping within the first 3)
  
- **$(1,3,2)$**: Inverse cycle → $[c,a,b,d]$
  - Element at position 1 moves 2 spots right the other way

- **$(2,3,4)$**: Cycles the last 3 positions → $[a,c,d,b]$
  - Element at position 2 moves 2 spots right (wrapping within the last 3)

- **$(2,4,3)$**: Inverse cycle → $[a,d,b,c]$
  - Element at position 2 moves 2 spots right the other way

## Step 3: Build the Graph Starting from Identity

**Analogy**: Think of this like exploring a maze where you start at the entrance (identity) and can move in 4 directions at each junction (applying our 4 generators).

Starting with **identity**: $[1,2,3,4]$

**Level 0** (distance 0 from identity):
- $[1,2,3,4]$

**Level 1** (apply each generator once):
- $(1,2,3)$: $[1,2,3,4] \to [2,3,1,4]$
- $(1,3,2)$: $[1,2,3,4] \to [3,1,2,4]$
- $(2,3,4)$: $[1,2,3,4] \to [1,3,4,2]$
- $(2,4,3)$: $[1,2,3,4] \to [1,4,2,3]$

**Level 2** (apply generators to Level 1 permutations):

From $[2,3,1,4]$:
- $(1,2,3) \to [3,1,2,4]$ (already seen!)
- $(1,3,2) \to [1,2,3,4]$ (back to identity!)
- $(2,3,4) \to [2,4,1,3]$ (new!)
- $(2,4,3) \to [2,1,3,4]$ (new!)

Continue this process for all Level 1 permutations...

## Step 4: Graph Structure

Since $k=3$ is odd, the generators produce only **even permutations** (the alternating group $A_4$). 

**Key fact**: $A_4$ has exactly **12 permutations** (half of the 24 permutations in $S_4$).

So **D(4,3)** will have:
- **12 vertices** (all even permutations of $[1,2,3,4]$)
- Each vertex has **degree 4** (connected by our 4 generators)
- The graph forms the **Cayley graph of $A_4$**
