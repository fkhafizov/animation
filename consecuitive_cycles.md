# Building D(4,3) Step by Step

Let me walk you through constructing the permutation graph **D(4,3)**, where we have $n=4$ elements and consecutive $k=3$ cycles.

## Step 1: Identify the Generators

For $n=4$ and $k=3$, the consecutive 3-cycles are:
- $(1,2,3)$ for $i=1$
- $(2,3,4)$ for $i=2$

And their inverses:
- $(1,2,3)^{-1} = (3,2,1) = (1,3,2)$
- $(2,3,4)^{-1} = (4,3,2) = (2,4,3)$

**So we have 4 operations total** that generate edges in our graph.

## Step 2: Understand What Each Generator Does

Think of a permutation as an arrangement, say $[a,b,c,d]$:

- **$(1,2,3)$**: Cycles the first 3 positions → $[b,c,a,d]$ 
  - Element at position 1 moves 2 spots right (wrapping within the first 3)
  
- **$(1,3,2)$**: Inverse cycle → $[c,a,b,d]$
  - Element at position 1 moves 2 spots right the other way

- **$(2,3,4)$**: Cycles the last 3 positions → $[a,c,d,b]$
  - Element at position 2 moves 2 spots right (wrapping within the last 3)

- **$(2,4,3)$**: Inverse cycle → $[a,d,b,c]$
  - Element at position 2 moves 2 spots right the other way

## Step 3: Build the Graph Starting from Identity

**Analogy**: Think of this like exploring a maze where you start at the entrance (identity) and can move in 4 directions at each junction (applying our 4 generators).

Starting with **identity**: $[1,2,3,4]$

**Level 0** (distance 0 from identity):
- $[1,2,3,4]$

**Level 1** (apply each generator once):
- $(1,2,3)$: $[1,2,3,4] \to [2,3,1,4]$
- $(1,3,2)$: $[1,2,3,4] \to [3,1,2,4]$
- $(2,3,4)$: $[1,2,3,4] \to [1,3,4,2]$
- $(2,4,3)$: $[1,2,3,4] \to [1,4,2,3]$

**Level 2** (apply generators to Level 1 permutations):

From $[2,3,1,4]$:
- $(1,2,3) \to [3,1,2,4]$ (already seen!)
- $(1,3,2) \to [1,2,3,4]$ (back to identity!)
- $(2,3,4) \to [2,4,1,3]$ (new!)
- $(2,4,3) \to [2,1,3,4]$ (new!)

Continue this process for all Level 1 permutations...

## Step 4: Graph Structure

Since $k=3$ is odd, the generators produce only **even permutations** (the alternating group $A_4$). 

**Key fact**: $A_4$ has exactly **12 permutations** (half of the 24 permutations in $S_4$).

So **D(4,3)** will have:
- **12 vertices** (all even permutations of $[1,2,3,4]$)
- Each vertex has **degree 4** (connected by our 4 generators)
- The graph forms the **Cayley graph of $A_4$**

# Q: are there formulas for computing vertices, edges, and diameters for D(n,k)?

# Formulas for D(n,k) Parameters

Great question! Here are the formulas we can derive:

## Number of Vertices: $|V|$

Based on the group theory:

$$|V(n,k)| = \begin{cases}
n! & \text{if } k \text{ is even (generates all of } S_n\text{)} \\
\frac{n!}{2} & \text{if } k \text{ is odd (generates } A_n\text{)}
\end{cases}$$

**Example**: D(4,3) has $\frac{4!}{2} = \frac{24}{2} = 12$ vertices ✓

## Degree of Each Vertex: $d$

The degree equals the number of distinct generators:

$$d(n,k) = \begin{cases}
n - 1 & \text{if } k = 2 \text{ (transpositions are self-inverse)} \\
2(n - k + 1) & \text{if } k > 2
\end{cases}$$

**Why?** 
- We have $(n-k+1)$ consecutive $k$-cycles: $(1,2,\ldots,k), (2,3,\ldots,k+1), \ldots, (n-k+1,\ldots,n)$
- Plus their $(n-k+1)$ inverses
- For $k=2$: $(i,i+1) = (i,i+1)^{-1}$, so no doubling

**Example**: D(4,3) has degree $2(4-3+1) = 2(2) = 4$ ✓

## Number of Edges: $|E|$

Since this is a Cayley graph (regular graph):

$$|E(n,k)| = \frac{|V| \times d}{2}$$

Substituting:

$$|E(n,k)| = \begin{cases}
\frac{n! \cdot (n-1)}{2} & \text{if } k = 2 \\
n! \cdot (n-k+1) & \text{if } k > 2 \text{ and } k \text{ even} \\
\frac{n!}{2} \cdot (n-k+1) & \text{if } k > 2 \text{ and } k \text{ odd}
\end{cases}$$

**Example**: D(4,3) has $\frac{24}{2} \cdot (4-3+1) = 12 \cdot 2 = 24$ edges

## Diameter: $\text{diam}$

This is the **tricky one** - no simple closed form for general $(n,k)$!

### Known Special Cases:

**For $k=2$ (Permutohedron / Cayley graph with Coxeter generators):**

$$\text{diam}(n,2) = \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$

This is the maximum number of inversions in a permutation. The furthest point from identity $[1,2,\ldots,n]$ is the reversal $[n,n-1,\ldots,1]$.

**For $k=3$ (generates $A_n$):**

The diameter grows roughly like $\binom{n}{2}$ but the exact formula is more complex. For small $n$:
- $\text{diam}(4,3) = 3$
- $\text{diam}(5,3) = 7$ 
- $\text{diam}(6,3) = 12$

**General bounds:**

$$\frac{n!}{2(n-k+1)} \leq \text{diam}(n,k) \leq \binom{n}{2}$$

(Lower bound from volume growth, upper bound from reducing to $k=2$ case)

## Summary Table

| Parameter | Formula |
|-----------|---------|
| Vertices | $n!$ (even $k$) or $\frac{n!}{2}$ (odd $k$) |
| Degree | $n-1$ ($k=2$) or $2(n-k+1)$ ($k>2$) |
| Edges | $\frac{\|V\| \cdot d}{2}$ |
| Diameter | $\binom{n}{2}$ for $k=2$; **open problem** for general $k$ |

**Analogy**: Think of diameter as "how many moves to reach the opposite corner of a Rubik's cube." For $k=2$ (simple swaps), we know exactly. For $k=3$ (3-cycles), it's like asking "God's number" for a specific puzzle variant - we can compute it case-by-case but lack a universal formula.
